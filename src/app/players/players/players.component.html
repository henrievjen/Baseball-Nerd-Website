How to Read a Player Profile

Opening any player brings up season-by-season and career totals, split into Hitting and Pitching where a player has both (a two-way player like a converted position-player-turned- reliever will show both tables). Four tabs control which slice of that data you're looking at, and each answers a different question:

Season shows the current year only — the view to use for "is this guy actually good right now," fantasy roster decisions, and anything about current form. Career rolls up every season a player has logged, which is the right lens for Hall of Fame arguments, milestone tracking, or judging whether a hot month is a real change in talent or a blip against a much larger sample. Game Log breaks the current season down game by game, which is where you'll actually spot a hitter in a slump or a starter who's lost command over his last three outings — season totals hide exactly that kind of recent trend. vs. Opponents filters a player's career numbers down to a single team, which is the split to check before a series if a hitter has historically owned (or been owned by) that opponent's pitching staff.

A word of caution on small samples: a hot vs.-opponent split built on 20 career at-bats is mostly noise, not a real skill difference — a player's true talent shows up much more reliably in a full season than in a handful of games against one team. The same logic applies to the Game Log view during any short stretch. Treat these splits as color, not as predictions.

Search works on partial names too — typing a last name alone will surface every matching player, active or retired, so you don't need the exact full spelling to find who you're looking for. If you're not sure who to look up, the leaders above are a good starting point, or check the full stats leaderboards for more categories than the two shown here.
