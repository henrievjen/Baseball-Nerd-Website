How to Evaluate a Starting Pitcher: The Stats That Actually Matter

By Henri Evjen  ·  Baseball Nerd  ·  10 min read

ERA is the first number anyone looks at for a starting pitcher, and it's also the most misleading one on its own. A 3.20 ERA can belong to a genuine ace or to an average pitcher who happened to have an excellent defense playing behind him all year. Telling those two pitchers apart is exactly what the rest of this guide is for.

Why ERA Alone Isn't Enough

Earned Run Average counts runs that score while a pitcher is on the mound, minus runs caused by fielding errors. The problem is what it still includes: every ball put in play that isn't an error is on the pitcher's ledger, whether his shortstop made an incredible diving stop or stood flat-footed and watched a catchable ball roll to the wall. A pitcher doesn't control his defense, his home park, or a good deal of plain luck on where batted balls happen to land. ERA bundles all of that together with the pitcher's actual stuff and command, and there's no way to separate them from the number alone.

This is exactly the problem FIP was built to solve.

FIP: Fielding Independent Pitching

FIP estimates what a pitcher's ERA "should" look like if you strip out everything his defense and luck contributed, and count only the outcomes a pitcher controls almost entirely by himself: strikeouts, walks, hit-by-pitches, and home runs allowed. It's built on the same scale as ERA, so a FIP of 3.50 reads the same way a 3.50 ERA would — which makes the two numbers directly comparable, and that comparison is the whole point.

When ERA and FIP are close together, a pitcher's results are backed up by his actual performance. When they diverge, that gap is worth paying attention to:

  • ERA well below FIP (say, a 3.00 ERA with a 4.20 FIP) usually means a pitcher has been getting help — a strong defense, favorable sequencing (allowing hits with nobody on base rather than in bunches), or simple good luck on batted balls. That gap tends to close the following season, which is why FIP is often a better predictor of next year's ERA than this year's ERA is.
  • ERA well above FIP points the other way — a pitcher who's actually missing bats and limiting walks but getting hurt by shaky defense or bad luck on balls in play. This is the pitcher most worth targeting in a trade or a fantasy waiver claim, since his underlying performance is better than his ugly ERA suggests.
Reading the Gap Between ERA and FIP
PatternWhat It Usually Means
ERA ≈ FIPResults match true performance
ERA < FIPGetting help from defense/luck — expect regression
ERA > FIPPitching better than results show — a buy-low candidate

WHIP: The Simplest Read on Baserunners

WHIP (walks plus hits per inning pitched) answers a blunt question: how many baserunners does this pitcher allow? A WHIP near 1.00 means, on average, one baserunner per inning — excellent control of the game. Anything creeping toward 1.40 or higher means a pitcher is constantly working from the stretch with runners on, which raises the odds of a big inning even if his ERA hasn't caught up to it yet. WHIP doesn't distinguish a leadoff walk from a two-out infield single, but as a quick gut-check on how much traffic a pitcher allows, it's hard to beat for simplicity.

Strikeout and Walk Rates: What a Pitcher Controls Most

Strikeout rate (usually shown as K/9, strikeouts per nine innings, or K%, the percentage of batters faced who struck out) and walk rate (BB/9 or BB%) get at the most fundamental pitcher skills: missing bats and throwing strikes. These are the two outcomes that involve the least defensive help and the least luck — a strikeout doesn't need a fielder to do anything, and a walk is entirely the pitcher's (and to a lesser extent, the umpire's) doing.

The two are often combined into K/BB ratio (strikeouts divided by walks), which is one of the stickiest, most predictive numbers in pitching. A pitcher running a 4:1 K/BB ratio or better is missing bats and pounding the strike zone at the same time — a combination that tends to produce good results in almost any context, at almost any park, against almost any lineup.

Innings and Durability: The Stat That Isn't a Rate

Every stat above is a rate — it describes performance per batter or per inning, and says nothing about how many innings a pitcher actually provides. A starter who throws 200 excellent innings gives his team something a starter who throws 120 very good innings and then gets hurt cannot: he keeps the bullpen rested all year and takes the ball every fifth day without fail. When you're comparing two starters with similar rate stats, the one who reliably gives his team six-plus innings a start is usually the more valuable pitcher, even if his per-inning numbers are a touch less shiny.

Context Still Matters: Park, Opponent, and Sample Size

None of these stats exist in a vacuum. A pitcher's home park changes what a "good" ERA or FIP looks like — someone pitching half his games at a hitter's park like Coors Field needs real context before you compare his raw numbers to a pitcher working in a pitcher-friendly park. Baseball Nerd's player profiles let you check a pitcher's vs. Opponents split and full Game Log, which is exactly where you'd look to see whether a strong season number is being carried by a couple of dominant starts against weak lineups, or is genuinely consistent outing to outing.

Small samples cut every direction. Three bad starts in April can wreck a full season's ERA before a pitcher settles in, and three great starts in September can flatter a mediocre year. The more innings and starts a stat is built on, the more you can trust it — a full season of FIP means a lot more than four starts of it.

If You Only Check Three Numbers

  1. FIP — the best single-number estimate of true pitching performance, independent of defense and luck.
  2. K/BB ratio — the clearest read on the two skills a pitcher controls almost entirely himself.
  3. Innings per start — how much value he actually provides beyond the numbers on the page.

ERA still matters — it's what actually gets a pitcher wins and shows up on his baseball card — but treat it as the headline, not the whole story.
